Photocurrent spectroscopy is a rather unconventional type of ultrafast pump-probe spectroscopy technique where instead of analyzing a probe beam containing information about a system one gathers information by analyzing a photocurrent. This photocurrent is either fully realized by laser excitation (photo-current spectroscopy), or an externally applied gating current is altered by modifying the materials conductivity by laser excitation (Photo-conductivity spectroscopy). There are many different variations of this technique, which has existed since the $1982$, but only became popular around $2012$ due to ..... The version we use is a far-field implementation of photocurrent cross-correlation spectroscopy.\\
\newline
In photocurrent cross-correlation spectroscopy one uses two closely spaced light pulses to successively excite a sample. The second pulse sees a different system than the first, which introduced a non-linear effect. This can be understood by investigating the simple example o f heating in an ideal electon gass. The first pulse sees the system as it is in the ground state and deposits thermal energy $E$ by absorption, raising the temperature by $\Delta T = C_{\text{e}}(T_{\text{e}})E$. The second pulse deposits the same amount of energy, but lifts the temperature of the electron gas by a lesser amount due to the temperature dependence of the electronic heat capacity $C_{\text{e}}(T_{\text{e}})\approx\gamma T_{\text{e}}$ at low temperatures.

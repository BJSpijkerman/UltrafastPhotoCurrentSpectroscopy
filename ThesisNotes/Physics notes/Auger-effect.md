The __Auger effect__ (also known as the _Meitner-Auger effect_) is a physical phenomenon in atomic physics where an excited atom releases excess energy by ejecting an electron rather than emitting a phonon
![[AugerEffect.jpg|328]]
## Core Mechanism
1. <b>Inner-Shell Vacancy</b>: An incident particle or high-energy photon knocks out an inner-shell electron (e.g. from the 1s core hole).
2. <b>De-excitation</b>: An electron from a higher energy level drops down to fill the inner vacancy.
3. <b>Electron Emission</b>: Instead of releasing this transition energy as an X-ray photon, the energy is radioationlessly transferred to another electron in an outer shell which is then kicked out of the atom. This secondary emitted electron is called an __Auger electron__.
![[Auger_Process.png]]
__Note on Atomic Weight__: For lighter elements (atomic number $Z < 12$), the Auger effect is the dominant relaxation path. For heavier elements, emitting an X-ray photon (X-ray fluorescence) becomes progressively more likely.

## History & Discovery
- <b>Lise Meitner</b>: First observed and published the phenomenon in 1922 during her research on nuclear beta decay spectra.
- <b>Pierre Victor Auger</b>: Independently discovered the effect in 1923 during his analysis of cloud chamber experiments, leading to a theoretical description based on quantum transitions.

## Applications & Implications
- <b>Auger Electron Spectroscopy (AES)</b>: A major surface analytical technique. By bombarding a material with X-rays or electron beams and measuring the energy spectrum of emitted Auger electrons, scientists can identify elements present on a sample's surface and understand their chemical enviroment.
- <b>Semiconductor Physics</b>: A related process known as [[Auger recombination]] occurs when an electron and a hole recombine, transferring energy directly to another conduction electron. The reverse process is known as [[Impact ionization]].
- <b>Biological Damage</b>: Following K-shell ionization in cellular atoms (like DNA), ejected  Auger electrons can break the suger-phosphate backbone, contributing to radiation damage in biological tissue.
